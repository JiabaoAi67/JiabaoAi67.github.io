A study of weight sharing, loop placement, and sampling budget in F5-TTS

Audio samples Results Page source

Abstract

Where should a flow-matching speech generator share weights across depth? We study seven F5-TTS layouts that execute the same 18 Transformer block calls, varying both the amount and position of sharing. Three partial loops—Prefix, Middle, and Suffix—have identical parameter counts but respond differently to the sampling budget. At 32 steps, their word error rates are close. At four steps, Suffix has 3.44 and 5.97 percentage points higher WER than Prefix on Seed-TTS and LibriSpeech-PC. Among the partial loops, Middle achieves the lowest 32-step WER on both datasets, while Suffix gives the highest 32-step speaker similarity among the partial loops. The four-step placement gap persists across training checkpoints despite Suffix’s lower logged training loss. Weight sharing also reduces storage: repeating nine blocks twice saves 47.1% of parameters and 34.5% of peak inference allocation, with comparable sampling time. An adjacent-repeat SEQUENCE control further shows that reuse order changes the balance between intelligibility and predicted naturalness. These results show that weight sharing should be evaluated at the intended sampling budget.

Sharing reduces storage while retaining the executed depth. CYCLE and SEQUENCE 9 × 2 each use 47.1% fewer parameters and 34.5% less peak inference allocation than the Baseline. Their inference allocation is therefore 65.5% of the Baseline; all seven models still execute 18 block calls per network evaluation.

The order of reuse changes the trade-off. At 32 steps, SEQUENCE lowers Seed-TTS WER from CYCLE’s 2.60 to 2.03, while UTMOS falls from 3.90 to 3.75. On LibriSpeech-PC, WER rises from 2.14 to 2.26. SEQUENCE has higher SIM-o on both datasets. Parameter count and executed depth are unchanged.

Training uses eight GPUs, accumulation of two, and BF16 mixed precision with FP32 parameters, EMA, and Adam states. Values are the largest rank-0 cumulative allocation peaks in records from 400k–500k updates. These include each process’s earlier allocation history and are neither maxima across all ranks nor minimum GPU capacities.

At the same 32-step inference setting, sampling RTF ranges from 0.1426 to 0.1440 across the seven layouts. RTF is total sampling time divided by generated audio duration, excluding vocoding. There is one recorded timing run per layout; these small differences do not establish a speed advantage. Detailed timing values remain in the result data.

There is one training run per layout. The comparison fixes 500k updates rather than selecting an earlier checkpoint for each model. Across nine common checkpoints, Suffix has lower logged training loss than Prefix but higher four-step WER on both test sets; the gap widens from 300k to 500k. Training loss uses online weights, while speech evaluation uses EMA. This observation does not establish a causal mechanism. No human MOS study has been completed for these models.
